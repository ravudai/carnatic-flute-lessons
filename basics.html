This lesson covers everything a beginner needs before their first recital piece: holding the flute, producing a clean Sa, the foundational Sarali and Janta Varisai exercises, the seven talas, and your very first Geetham, Varnam and Kriti. Swara notation is simplified for teaching purposes — always check against your teacher's notation for the authoritative version.

1. Getting Started with the Flute

Choosing your flute

Beginners usually start on either an E♯ scale (madhyama sruti) flute or a D scale (also called the "concert" length) flute. The E♯ flute is shorter, needs less breath pressure, and is the most common recommendation for new students. A longer D or C scale flute is typically taken up later once breath control and finger stretch improve.

Posture

  • Sit cross-legged on the floor (or upright in a chair) with a straight spine.
  • Hold the flute horizontally, angled very slightly downward.
  • Keep both elbows relaxed and away from the body — don't hunch the shoulders.
  • Keep the head level; turn slightly toward the flute rather than dropping the chin.

Holding the flute

  • The left hand covers the three holes closer to the blow hole (nearest the mouth).
  • The right hand covers the remaining holes further from the blow hole.
  • Use the pads of the fingers (not the tips) to fully seal each hole — no air should leak.
  • Rest the flute lightly on the base of the index fingers and the chin/lower lip; don't grip tightly.

Producing your first Sa (tone production)

  1. Close all finger holes for the base "Sa" fingering for your flute.
  2. Rest the blow hole gently against the lower lip, roughly covering half of it.
  3. Shape the lips into a small, narrow opening (as if saying "phoo" softly).
  4. Blow a slow, steady stream of air across (not into) the hole.
  5. Adjust the angle and lip position until you hear a clear, steady tone rather than air noise.
  6. Practice holding this single Sa for as long and as steadily as possible — this is the foundation of good tone (nada) for everything that follows.

Daily long-tone practice on Sa (and later Pa) builds the breath control every subsequent exercise depends on.

2. The 7 Notes & Fingering (Sa Ri Ga Ma Pa Da Ni)

These diagrams use the conventional 6-hole teaching bansuri — the standard beginner instrument model, with six finger holes plus the embouchure (blow) hole. Holes are numbered 1 to 6, with hole 1 closest to the embouchure (played by the left hand's first finger) and hole 6 farthest away, nearest the right hand's little finger. A filled/dark circle means the hole is closed (covered); an open/outlined circle means it is lifted (open). All seven notes below start from the same all-closed Sa and progressively lift fingers from hole 6 towards hole 1, the simplified pattern most beginner courses teach for the major/Shankarabharanam-equivalent scale.

Sa

All six holes covered — your resting position.

Ri

Lift only the last finger (hole 6, farthest from your mouth).

Ga

Lift the last two fingers (holes 5–6).

Ma

Lift the last three fingers (holes 4–6).

Pa

Lift the last four fingers (holes 3–6).

Da

Lift all but the very first finger (holes 2–6).

Ni

Lift every finger — only your lower lip seals the embouchure.

3. Daily Long Note (Sa Sadhana) Routine

Before moving on to the Sarali Varisai exercises, spend time every day holding each of the seven notes as a long, steady tone. This single habit — often called sa sadhana — builds the breath control, embouchure steadiness and pitch accuracy that every later exercise depends on.

Step-by-step

  1. Sit or stand with good posture (see the Posture section above).
  2. Take a slow, deep breath from the diaphragm, keeping the shoulders relaxed.
  3. Play Sa and hold it as long and as steadily as possible, without the pitch or volume wavering.
  4. Rest for a breath, then repeat for Ri, Ga, Ma, Pa, Da and Ni — one note at a time.
  5. Once you reach Ni, come back down: hold Da, Pa, Ma, Ga, Ri and Sa again, descending.

Suggested duration: start by holding each note for 8–10 seconds, and build up to 20–30 seconds per note over about two weeks of daily practice.

Daily target: 10–15 minutes total — for example, 3 full rounds of all seven notes ascending and then descending.

Tip: focus on diaphragm breathing, a steady embouchure, and avoiding any wobble in pitch or volume. If you have a tuner app, use it to check that each note stays perfectly steady for the whole duration.

7. First Geetham — "Sri Gananatha" ( Raga Malahari, Tala Rupaka)

Geethams are simple, song-like pieces sung/played entirely in one speed, without elaborate ornamentation — a gentle first step from pure swara exercises into sahitya (lyrics). "Sri Gananatha" invokes Lord Ganesha and is traditionally the very first Geetham taught. Tala: Rupaka (3 beats: 1 + 2).

8. First Varnam — "Ninnukori" ( Raga Mohanam, Tala Adi)

Varnams are more advanced practice pieces that build stamina, speed control and raga bhava (mood). "Ninnukori" in the pentatonic Raga Mohanam (S R G P D) is one of the most common first varnams taught to students.

Pallavi (opening line) — simplified

Ninnukoriyunnadira

Practice tip — the 3 speeds (kaalam): Varnams are traditionally practiced in three progressively faster speeds using the same tala cycle: first speed (each swara gets a full beat), second speed (double the notes per beat), and third speed (four times the notes per beat). Always master a stable first speed before attempting second or third speed — use the Adi Tala metronome above and simply play more notes within each click as you speed up your internal subdivision.

9. First Kriti — "Vatapi Ganapatim" ( Raga Hamsadhwani, Tala Adi)

Kritis are devotional compositions with pallavi, anupallavi and charanam sections, rich in raga bhava and often taken up for concert performance. "Vatapi Ganapatim" (composed by Muthuswami Dikshitar) in the pentatonic Raga Hamsadhwani (S R G P N) is a landmark first kriti for most students.
